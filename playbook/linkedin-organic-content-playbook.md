# LinkedIn Organic Content Playbook for B2B SaaS

**100Hires Portfolio Assignment — Stage 2**  
**Topic:** LinkedIn Organic Content Strategy for B2B SaaS  
**Research base:** 10 practitioners researched in Stage 1 + verified source-level analysis  
**Author:** Chetana Rohilla

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The Stage 1 research identified a recurring pattern across leading LinkedIn practitioners: successful organic content is less about publishing generic advice and more about earning attention, demonstrating expertise, building trust, and creating a reason for the audience to keep listening.

However, the experts do not agree on exactly how to do this.

Some emphasize hooks and distribution. Others prioritize stories, zero-click value, customer insight, or commercially meaningful points of view. Some use AI aggressively as a productivity layer, while others warn that AI-generated writing can erase the human qualities that make content distinctive.

Based on the research, this playbook takes the following position:

> **For B2B SaaS, optimize each post for credible expertise rather than engagement alone.**

A strong post should do four things:

1. **Earn attention** with a specific problem, tension, observation, or claim.
2. **Demonstrate expertise** through evidence, experience, customer insight, or a defensible point of view.
3. **Deliver standalone value** so the reader does not need to click elsewhere to benefit.
4. **Create a next step** through conversation, follow-up content, a useful resource, or a relevant product/business connection.

This produces a practical content system:

**Audience → Problem → Point of View → Proof → Teaching → Conversation → Measurement → Iteration**

The playbook deliberately does **not** treat any expert's framework as universally correct. The recommendations below are adaptations for an early-stage B2B SaaS team, not claims that one creator's system works for every business.

### How Recommendations Are Sourced

Each recommendation in this playbook is classified into one of three categories:

**Source-derived recommendation** — directly adapted from a specific practitioner's documented advice. The original source is cited immediately after the recommendation.

**Research synthesis** — a conclusion that emerged from recurring patterns across multiple Stage 1 sources. The relevant sources are cited together.

**Original operating recommendation** — my own proposed adaptation for an early-stage B2B SaaS team. These are explicitly labeled as original rather than attributed to an expert.

This distinction is intentional. The goal is not to make expert opinions appear to be empirical facts, but to separate observed evidence, practitioner advice, and my own judgment.

## 2. Who This Playbook Is For

This SOP is designed for:

- B2B SaaS founders
- Early-stage SaaS marketers
- Founder-led marketing teams
- B2B content marketers with limited resources
- Subject-matter experts building a professional audience
- Small marketing teams using LinkedIn as an organic distribution channel

It is most useful when the business has at least some access to:

- customer conversations,
- product usage,
- sales calls,
- implementation experience,
- experiments,
- internal expertise,
- customer questions,
- or original market observations.

If none of these exist, the proof component of the strategy becomes much weaker.

# 3. The Core Strategy

## 3.1 The operating principle

**Do not start with "What should we post?"**

Start with:

> **"What do we know because we are close to the problem that our audience does not know yet?"**

This changes the content process from content production to expertise capture.

The research supports this direction from several angles:

- Alex Hormozi's research emphasizes proof, demonstration, real work, audits, and customer outcomes.
- Daniel Murray's research emphasizes unique perspectives, niche authority, and real audience questions.
- Dave Gerhardt's research emphasizes human experience, storytelling, and original thinking in an AI-heavy content environment.
- Lara Acosta emphasizes documenting experiences and turning stories into educational content.
- Amanda Natividad emphasizes standalone, native value rather than forcing every interaction toward a click.
- Ross Simmonds emphasizes distribution and reuse rather than treating every content asset as disposable.

# 4. The Content Creation SOP

### Step 1 — Define the audience

Start with a specific audience hypothesis rather than trying to define the perfect audience before publishing.

Katelyn Bourgoin recommends starting with a hypothesis, creating content, and using audience feedback to refine both the audience and topic rather than waiting for complete clarity upfront.

Source: Katelyn Bourgoin, "I've built an audience of over 265,000 people...", LinkedIn, 11.04.2024:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/katebour_ive-built-an-audience-of-over-265000-people-activity-7184193526056833024-na9j

### Chetana's adaptation

For an early-stage B2B SaaS team, I would operationalize this as:

1. Start with one narrow audience hypothesis.
2. Publish against that hypothesis.
3. Monitor the quality of audience response.
4. Refine the audience definition based on evidence.

Examples:

- Seed-stage B2B SaaS founders
- RevOps leaders at 50–200 employee SaaS companies
- Product marketers launching new SaaS products
- Founders trying to improve activation

Avoid starting with an audience as broad as "B2B marketers."

The narrower hypothesis makes it easier to identify a specific problem and develop a defensible point of view.

Source: Chetana Rohilla — original operating adaptation based on the Stage 1 research.

---

## Step 2 — Build a problem bank

Collect problems from:

- customer calls
- sales objections
- support questions
- onboarding friction
- product analytics
- lost deals
- customer reviews
- founder observations
- industry changes
- experiments

Create a spreadsheet with:

| Date | Audience | Problem | Evidence | Potential Post |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01/07 | SaaS founders | Low activation | 12 customer interviews | "Your onboarding problem may not be onboarding" |
| 03/07 | RevOps | Poor handoff | Sales call notes | "The CRM isn't the problem" |

The purpose is to prevent generic topic selection.

---

## Step 3 — Select the content thesis

For each post, write one sentence:

> **"I believe X because Y."**

Examples:

- "I believe SaaS onboarding should optimize for time-to-value, not number of completed steps, because users don't care about your process."
- "I believe most B2B content fails because it explains the category instead of showing how the company thinks differently."
- "I believe customer objections are better content prompts than keyword tools."

If the thesis cannot be written in one sentence, the post is probably trying to do too much.

---

## Step 4 — Choose the evidence

Prioritize evidence in this order:

1. First-party customer evidence
2. Product/experiment data
3. Specific real-world experience
4. Original analysis
5. Expert evidence
6. General industry observation

Do not present an expert's opinion as if it were your own empirical finding.

Use labels such as:

> "In our customer interviews..."

or

> "My interpretation of this pattern is..."

or

> "Chris Walker argues..."

This distinction improves source skepticism.

# 5. The Post Structure

## The recommended structure

### 1. Attention

Open with:

- a specific problem,
- a surprising result,
- a strong observation,
- a contrarian claim,
- or a personal moment.

Justin Welsh explicitly recommends a specific sub-niche, a scroll-stopping first line, curiosity, rapid teaching, and a clear recap.

**Source:** Justin Welsh, *How to write viral posts on LinkedIn*, LinkedIn, 30.06.2021: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_how-to-write-viral-posts-on-linkedin-1-activity-6815975446891503616-8L8I

### 2. Proof

Immediately establish why the reader should listen.

Possible proof:

- customer result
- experiment
- failure
- observed pattern
- product data
- specific experience
- original analysis

### 3. Teaching

Give the reader something they can apply.

Possible formats:

- 3-step process
- checklist
- framework
- before/after
- case study
- teardown
- decision tree

### 4. Application

Tell the reader how to use the idea.

### 5. Conversation

End with a question only when there is a genuine reason to discuss the issue.

Do not add a fake question solely to increase comments.

# 6. Hook SOP

Justin Welsh recommends beginning with a specific sub-niche, a scroll-stopping first line, and a curiosity-driven hook before quickly teaching the reader something useful.

Source: Justin Welsh, "How to write viral posts on LinkedIn," LinkedIn, 30.06.2021:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_how-to-write-viral-posts-on-linkedin-1-activity-6815975446891503616-8L8I

### Chetana's operating adaptation

For this playbook, I would operationalize the hook process as follows:

1. Write 10 possible first lines.
2. Score each from 1–5 on:
   - Specificity to the audience
   - Curiosity
   - Clarity of promise
   - Connection to a real insight
   - Avoidance of generic jargon
3. Select the strongest candidate.
4. Before publishing, verify that the hook accurately represents the evidence contained in the post.

Source: Chetana Rohilla — original operating adaptation based primarily on Justin Welsh's hook framework and the Stage 1 research.

# 7. Storytelling SOP

Storytelling should be used as a **vehicle for expertise**, not as an end in itself.

Lara Acosta's Educational Storyteller framework combines a broad idea, personal story, and lesson. Her later storytelling framework similarly emphasizes using a story to introduce a lesson and actionable advice.

**Source:** Lara Acosta, *Everyone on LinkedIn should be telling stories*, LinkedIn, 24.07.2024: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_everyone-on-linkedin-should-be-telling-stories-activity-7221839528632758272-jVMq  

**Source:** Lara Acosta, *My 4-step storytelling framework is unbeatable*, LinkedIn: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_my-4-step-storytelling-framework-is-unbeatable-activity-7287446692763742208-aC5s

### Use this structure

**Context → Tension → What changed → Lesson → Application**

Example:

> We assumed customers were abandoning onboarding because it was too long.
>
> We shortened it.
>
> Activation barely moved.
>
> Then we watched five sessions and found the real problem: users couldn't tell what "done" looked like.
>
> The lesson: removing steps is not the same as removing uncertainty.
>
> If your activation is weak, watch the user's decision points before redesigning the flow.

The story gives the reader a reason to care. The lesson gives the story business value.

# 8. Educational Content SOP

Use educational posts for:

- frameworks
- checklists
- decision criteria
- customer research
- teardown analyses
- lessons from experiments
- implementation guides

A useful rule:

> **One post = one job for the reader.**

Do not combine:

- SEO strategy
- LinkedIn strategy
- product positioning
- demand generation
- AI tools

into one post.

Instead, create five posts.

# 9. Proof-Driven Content

This is one of the strongest conclusions from the Stage 1 research.

Generic advice is increasingly easy to reproduce.

Proof is harder to reproduce.

### Preferred formats

**Customer teardown**

> "We reviewed 12 onboarding flows. Here's the pattern."

**Experiment**

> "We tested two onboarding emails. Here's what happened."

**Live audit**

> "I audited this public pricing page. Here's what I would change."

**Behind the scenes**

> "Here's how our team decides which leads deserve sales attention."

**Case study**

> "A customer came to us with X. We changed Y. Here's what we learned."

Hormozi's supplied YouTube research specifically emphasizes documenting actual work, customer interactions, audits, walkthroughs, and real-world outcomes as content assets.

**Source:** Alex Hormozi, *The New way of making content in the Age of AI*, YouTube: https://youtu.be/XsWSvz-aewA

# 10. Zero-Click Value

Do not make every LinkedIn post a teaser.

Amanda Natividad's zero-click thesis argues for native content that provides standalone value without requiring the reader to click away.

**Source:** Amanda Natividad, *In a nutshell, here are the huge problems we're dealing with in marketing today*, LinkedIn, 17.05.2024: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amandanat_in-a-nutshell-here-are-the-huge-problems-activity-7197278523311415296-Bjb-  

**Source:** Amanda Natividad, *Zero Click Content Era: AI Overviews Impact on Search Behavior*, LinkedIn: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amandanat_nobodys-clicking-anymore-and-its-not-activity-7414697334942576640-p_NS

### Our adaptation

> **Give away the insight. Gate the implementation asset.**

Example:

LinkedIn post:

> "5 mistakes SaaS teams make in onboarding."

Give all five mistakes.

Optional next step:

> "I've turned this into a 20-point onboarding audit. Comment 'audit' and I'll send it."

The post remains useful even if the reader never comments or clicks.

# 11. Distribution SOP

Creating the post is only half the system.

Ross Simmonds explicitly argues that content needs distribution and advocates reusing strong content rather than constantly creating from zero.

**Source:** Ross Simmonds, *Why content distribution is more important than quality*, LinkedIn: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/rosssimmonds_contentstrategy-activity-7312122942966898688-GlSR  

**Source:** Ross Simmonds, *Create once. Distribute forever.*, LinkedIn: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/rosssimmonds_create-once-distribute-forever-activity-7299818377194164226-IfLG  

**Source:** Justin Welsh, *How to turn 1 idea into 6 pieces of relevant content*, LinkedIn, 19.07.2022: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_how-to-turn-1-idea-into-6-pieces-of-relevant-activity-6955132767605035008-5h9k

### Repurposing workflow

One strong insight can become:

1. LinkedIn text post
2. LinkedIn carousel
3. Newsletter section
4. Short video
5. Long-form article
6. Sales enablement insight
7. Customer-facing checklist

Do not copy-paste the same post everywhere.

Change the format and framing for the platform.

# 12. AI SOP

AI should be used as a **production assistant**, not the source of expertise.

Dave Gerhardt's research stresses the importance of human, authentic, experience-driven content in an AI-heavy environment. Justin Welsh's research shows a more permissive use of AI for brainstorming hooks, organizing thoughts, and preparing emails.

**Source:** Dave Gerhardt, *What content stands out in a sea of AI?*, LinkedIn, 23.06.2025: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/davegerhardt_what-content-stands-out-in-a-sea-of-ai-activity-7342936493310550016-jxtH  

**Source:** Justin Welsh, *ChatGPT will not miraculously grow your audience or business*, LinkedIn, 20.01.2023: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_chatgpt-will-not-miraculously-grow-your-audience-activity-7022189688861937664-KVzT

### Good AI use

- brainstorm hooks
- organize interview notes
- cluster customer problems
- turn a transcript into draft ideas
- identify repeated themes
- generate alternative headlines
- repurpose an existing insight

### Poor AI use

- inventing customer stories
- inventing data
- generating opinions without evidence
- publishing generic AI-written posts unchanged
- replacing subject-matter expertise

### Human-in-the-loop rule

**AI can accelerate the workflow. The human must own the claim.**

# 13. Measurement Framework

The Stage 1 research suggests that engagement alone is insufficient for evaluating B2B content. Chris Walker provides the clearest example: he argues that some posts that perform poorly on likes and views can generate more revenue because they introduce new thinking rather than reinforce existing beliefs.

Source: Chris Walker, "Fun fact: My worst performing LinkedIn posts drive the most revenue," LinkedIn, 06.01.2023:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chriswalker171_b2b-marketing-content-activity-7017125539991740416-X69p

### Chetana's measurement model

Based on that insight, I propose measuring content across three layers:

## Layer 1 — Attention

- impressions
- profile views
- average engagement rate
- follower growth

## Layer 2 — Quality

- saves
- meaningful comments
- comments from target accounts
- profile visits from ICP
- inbound conversations

## Layer 3 — Business

- qualified conversations
- demo requests
- newsletter signups
- influenced opportunities
- pipeline/revenue where attribution is available

Chris Walker's research provides an important warning: a post can underperform on engagement while producing more commercial value than a high-engagement post.

**Source:** Chris Walker, *Fun fact: My worst performing LinkedIn posts drive the most revenue*, LinkedIn, 06.01.2023: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chriswalker171_b2b-marketing-content-activity-7017125539991740416-X69p

### Recommended decision rule

Do not kill a topic because it received fewer likes.

Ask:

> **Did it reach the right people, create useful conversations, or produce evidence that the thesis is worth pursuing?**

# 14. Where Experts Disagree

## Disagreement 1 — Optimize for engagement vs optimize for commercial signal

### Author A: Justin Welsh

Justin recommends a structure built around attention: a specific sub-niche, strong hook, curiosity, fast teaching, a recap, and active engagement with comments.

**Source:** Justin Welsh, *How to write viral posts on LinkedIn*, 30.06.2021: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_how-to-write-viral-posts-on-linkedin-1-activity-6815975446891503616-8L8I

### Author B: Chris Walker

Chris argues that some of his lowest-performing posts by likes/views generated the most revenue because they introduced new ideas that challenged established thinking. He contrasts these with high-engagement posts that mostly reinforce beliefs people already hold.

**Source:** Chris Walker, *Fun fact: My worst performing LinkedIn posts drive the most revenue*, 06.01.2023: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chriswalker171_b2b-marketing-content-activity-7017125539991740416-X69p

### My decision

**Optimize for relevance and commercial signal first; use engagement as a diagnostic, not the final objective.**

Why:

A B2B SaaS team can waste time producing highly agreeable content that attracts likes but does not influence buying behavior.

However, completely ignoring attention would also be a mistake because nobody can learn from content they never consume.

Therefore:

**Attention gets the post read. Relevance gets it remembered. Proof gets it trusted.**

---

## Disagreement 2 — What should carry the post: story or information?

### Author A: Lara Acosta

Lara Acosta places storytelling at the front of the post. Her Educational Storyteller framework uses a personal story to create relatability and a lesson to retain educational value. Her later SLAY framework similarly positions the story as the hook and the lesson/actionable advice as the reason the reader stays.

Sources:

Lara Acosta, "Everyone on LinkedIn should be telling stories," LinkedIn:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_everyone-on-linkedin-should-be-telling-stories-activity-7221839528632758272-jVMq

Lara Acosta, "My 4-step storytelling framework is unbeatable," LinkedIn:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_my-4-step-storytelling-framework-is-unbeatable-activity-7287446692763742208-aC5s

### Author B: Amanda Natividad

Amanda Natividad's zero-click approach places the emphasis on giving the audience standalone value directly on the platform rather than making the social post primarily a vehicle for driving clicks elsewhere.

Source: Amanda Natividad, "In a nutshell, here are the huge problems we're dealing with in marketing today," LinkedIn, 17.05.2024:
https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amandanat_in-a-nutshell-here-are-the-huge-problems-activity-7197278523311415296-Bjb-

### My decision

I side with Amanda on the minimum requirement and with Lara on the optional delivery mechanism.

The post must provide standalone value. Storytelling should be used when it makes that value more memorable, credible, or emotionally relevant.

Therefore:

**Value is mandatory. Storytelling is conditional.**

For a B2B SaaS team, I would use:

- Story-led posts when the experience itself provides evidence or context.
- Direct educational posts when the subject is better explained through a framework, checklist, teardown, or analysis.

Source: Chetana Rohilla — original synthesis based on the two approaches above.

## Disagreement 3 — AI as a content accelerator vs AI as a threat to differentiation

### Author A: Justin Welsh

Justin describes practical uses for ChatGPT including brainstorming hooks, organizing thoughts/writing, and preparing emails.

**Source:** Justin Welsh, *ChatGPT will not miraculously grow your audience or business*, 20.01.2023: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/justinwelsh_chatgpt-will-not-miraculously-grow-your-audience-activity-7022189688861937664-KVzT

### Author B: Dave Gerhardt

Dave argues that content that feels authentic, real, and human stands out in a feed increasingly filled with AI-generated material. He emphasizes human experience and authenticity as differentiation.

**Source:** Dave Gerhardt, *What content stands out in a sea of AI?*, 23.06.2025: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/davegerhardt_what-content-stands-out-in-a-sea-of-ai-activity-7342936493310550016-jxtH

### My decision

**Use AI aggressively behind the scenes, but conservatively in the final voice.**

AI should reduce low-value production work.

It should not manufacture the core insight.

Recommended workflow:

**Human experience → AI organization → Human judgment → Human edit → Publish**

This gets the efficiency benefit without turning the feed into interchangeable AI copy.

# 15. What I Rejected and Why

## Rejected idea 1 — Optimize for virality as the primary goal

Some of the researched frameworks are explicitly framed around writing viral posts.

I rejected virality as the primary KPI for B2B SaaS.

### Why

Viral reach can be useful, but it can also attract people outside the ideal customer profile.

Chris Walker's research provides a strong counterpoint: some posts with lower engagement generated more revenue because they introduced new thinking.

**Source:** Chris Walker, 06.01.2023: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chriswalker171_b2b-marketing-content-activity-7017125539991740416-X69p

### What I kept

Hooks and attention optimization.

### What I rejected

The assumption that maximum engagement automatically means maximum business value.

---

## Rejected idea 2 — Put a CTA on every post

The Stage 1 research identified lead magnets, newsletters, and promotional mechanisms as common ways creators monetize attention.

I rejected the idea of making every post push the reader toward a conversion action.

### Why

Amanda Natividad's zero-click approach suggests that standalone value can itself build awareness, trust, and affinity.

Chris Walker's demand-generation example similarly describes content that educates without a sales CTA.

**Source:** Amanda Natividad, 17.05.2024: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amandanat_in-a-nutshell-here-are-the-huge-problems-activity-7197278523311415296-Bjb-  

**Source:** Chris Walker, *Lead Generation vs. Demand Generation*, LinkedIn: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/chriswalker171_marketing-b2b-demandgeneration-activity-6717782990421807104-z94Q

### What I kept

Use CTAs when there is a legitimate next step.

### What I rejected

The assumption that every valuable post needs to extract a click, comment, or lead.

# 16. My Original Ideas

## 16.1 The Expertise → Evidence → Application Framework

This framework is my synthesis/original operating model based on the research.

Every B2B SaaS post should contain three layers:

### 1. Expertise

What do you believe?

### 2. Evidence

Why should the reader believe you?

Examples:

- customer observation
- experiment
- data
- case study
- failure
- implementation experience

### 3. Application

What should the reader do differently?

### Example

**Weak:**

> "Customer onboarding is important."

**Better:**

> "Most onboarding problems aren't caused by too many steps."

**Best:**

> "We watched 12 onboarding sessions and found that users were not confused by the number of steps. They were confused about what success looked like. We changed the guidance around the first value moment rather than removing steps. Here's the three-part framework we now use."

The third version gives:

**Expertise → Evidence → Application**

This is particularly useful for B2B SaaS because it makes expertise visible instead of merely asserted.

---

## 16.2 The Proof-to-Content Loop

This is a second original operating idea.

Instead of treating content as a separate marketing task:

**Customer work → insight → content → audience feedback → better customer work**

Example:

1. Customer repeatedly asks the same question.
2. Team investigates the underlying problem.
3. Team documents what it learns.
4. Insight becomes a LinkedIn post.
5. Audience comments reveal additional edge cases.
6. Those edge cases inform the next customer conversation.
7. The improved insight becomes another content asset.

This creates a feedback loop between customer understanding and content.

The idea is inspired by the Stage 1 research on proof-based content, audience feedback, and documentation, but the loop itself is my proposed operating model.

# 17. Weaknesses of This Playbook

## 17.1 Engagement is not causation

High-performing posts may correlate with audience growth without causing it.

The research cannot prove that a specific hook, story structure, or posting pattern caused a business outcome.

---

## 17.2 Survivorship bias

The ten experts were selected because they are visible and successful.

We do not have data on:

- failed creators,
- abandoned strategies,
- unsuccessful posts,
- strategies that worked for a short period and stopped working.

Therefore, the research is biased toward observable winners.

---

## 17.3 Audience mismatch

A strategy used by a creator with hundreds of thousands of followers may behave differently for a SaaS founder with 2,000 followers.

Follower scale changes:

- distribution,
- social proof,
- comment velocity,
- content expectations,
- network effects.

---

## 17.4 Business-model differences

Some experts monetize:

- courses
- communities
- consulting
- personal brands
- media

A B2B SaaS company monetizes a product with a different buying journey.

Therefore, a creator's lead-generation framework cannot automatically be copied into SaaS.

---

## 17.5 Attribution is incomplete

A LinkedIn post may influence a buyer without receiving last-click credit.

Someone might:

1. see a post,
2. remember the company,
3. visit later,
4. talk to a colleague,
5. search for the company,
6. eventually become a customer.

The final conversion may not be attributable to the original post.

---

## 17.6 AI is changing the environment

Some recommendations in this playbook are based on content behavior that can change as LinkedIn algorithms, AI search, and audience behavior evolve.

The playbook should therefore be treated as an experimentation system, not permanent doctrine.

# 18. Who I Would NOT Recommend Following — and Why

## Alex Hormozi — as a complete operating model

I would **not recommend that a B2B SaaS marketer copy Alex Hormozi's content system wholesale.**

This is not a judgment that his content is ineffective.

The problem is transferability.

Hormozi's research is particularly strong around:

- proof,
- simple frameworks,
- demonstration,
- authority,
- content volume,
- business outcomes.

Those principles are useful.

But copying the entire style can create three problems for B2B SaaS:

### 1. Context mismatch

A founder selling a complex B2B product to multiple stakeholders may need more nuance than a highly compressed, high-energy business lesson.

### 2. Audience mismatch

A content style optimized for broad entrepreneurial attention is not automatically optimized for a narrow ICP such as CFOs, RevOps leaders, CTOs, or enterprise buyers.

### 3. Brand risk

Aggressive certainty and highly simplified claims can produce attention but may reduce credibility when the buying decision requires nuance and evidence.

### What I would borrow

**Proof → simplicity → frameworks → demonstration**

### What I would not copy

**Tone → volume → certainty → broad-audience positioning**

The principle is:

> **Borrow the underlying mechanism, not the surface style.**

# 19. Weekly Content Operating System

The following five-day structure is not presented as a frequency proven by the research. It is my proposed operating cadence for testing the major content types identified across the Stage 1 research.

Source: Chetana Rohilla — original operating recommendation based on recurring themes from Justin Welsh, Lara Acosta, Amanda Natividad, Chris Walker, Alex Hormozi, Dave Gerhardt, and Ross Simmonds.

### Monday — Customer insight

Publish:
- customer problem
- misconception
- observation
- lesson

### Tuesday — Education

Publish:
- framework
- checklist
- teardown
- how-to

### Wednesday — Point of view

Publish:
- strong opinion
- contrarian observation
- industry change

### Thursday — Proof

Publish:
- experiment
- case study
- behind-the-scenes
- data

### Friday — Story

Publish:
- failure
- lesson
- transformation
- personal experience

This is a testing cadence, not a universal recommendation. Performance should determine whether the cadence should be maintained, reduced, or changed.

# 20. 30-Day Experiment Plan

### Important distinction

The 30-day experiment below is not presented as a framework prescribed by any one of the ten experts.

Compare:

- broad vs niche topic

- another LinkedIn post

Then document what happened.

# 21. Post Quality Checklist

Before publishing, ask:

### Audience
- Is this clearly for a specific audience?
- Does the problem matter to them?

### Attention
- Does the first line create a reason to continue?
- Is it specific rather than generic?

### Expertise
- What do I know that the reader may not?
- Is there evidence?

### Value
- Can the reader use this without clicking elsewhere?
- Is there a concrete takeaway?

### Writing
- Is there one main idea?
- Can any sentence be removed?
- Is the language simple?
- Does it sound like a human?

### Business relevance
- Does this reinforce what we want to be known for?
- Would the right buyer recognize themselves in it?

### Integrity
- Are claims supported?
- Did I separate evidence from interpretation?
- Did I avoid inventing results?

# 22. Final Operating Principle

The research began with a question:

> **How do successful B2B SaaS practitioners grow through LinkedIn organic content?**

The answer is not simply:

> "Post consistently."

It is:

> **Become useful, become credible, and make that credibility visible repeatedly.**

The best content system is therefore not a collection of templates.

It is a system for converting:

**real work → real insight → useful content → audience feedback → better work**

That is the core operating loop this playbook recommends.
